TSP: tours and moats

Upper bound = a tour.  Lower bound = moats every tour must cross twice (the dual of the subtour LP).

A tour is an upper bound on the shortest round trip — but how do we convince anybody that no tour is shorter? Draw moats: a zone around a city, or around a group of cities. Any tour has to enter and leave every zone, so it crosses every zone at least twice. If the zones are drawn so that no edge is charged more than its length, then twice the total width of all zones is a lower bound. Finding the widest such moats is a linear program (the dual of the subtour-elimination relaxation); its optimum is the Held–Karp bound. Hover zones and edges for details.

Why is this a lower bound? — the argument, and where it comes from

The certificate. Fix a family of city sets S and give each a moat of width yS ≥ 0. Charge every edge e = {u,v} the widths of all moats it has to cross, i.e. those with exactly one endpoint inside: ΣS : e ∈ δ(S) yS. The picture is valid if no edge is charged more than its length ce. A tour visits the inside and the outside of every zone, so it uses at least two edges of every δ(S); summing the charges over the tour, length(tour) ≥ Σe ∈ tour charge(e) ≥ 2 ΣS yS.

The drawing. A zone for S is the set of points within distance yS of the zones already drawn for the cities in S. Because offsetting distributes over unions, every zone is simply a union of discs around its cities, with radius the sum of the widths on the way out — so nested (laminar) families draw as nested bands. Validity guarantees that the bands of disjoint sets do not overlap (for Euclidean instances exactly; for rounded or arbitrary distances the picture is a metaphor and discs may touch or overlap a little). A zone whose set is drawn "inside out" (the complement, shift-click) describes the same cut and the same charge.

The LP. Maximising 2 Σ yS subject to the edge budgets is the dual of the cut relaxation of the TSP, min Σ cexe s.t. x(δ(S)) ≥ 2 for all ∅ ≠ S ⊊ V, x ≥ 0. For metric distances this has the same value as the subtour-elimination relaxation with degree constraints x(δ(v)) = 2, i.e. the Held–Karp bound (parsimonious property of Goemans and Bertsimas). Its dual variables for cities may be negative, which has no moat picture; on a non-metric instance the page reports that bound separately. There is always an optimal dual that is laminar (uncrossing: δ(A)+δ(B) ≥ δ(A∩B)+δ(A∪B)), so the picture never needs overlapping bands. The page solves the LP by column generation: cuts are added as columns while a global minimum cut of the fractional x is below 2.

Gaps. The tour is an upper bound, the LP a lower bound; the true optimum lies in between. Even the exact optimum can exceed the LP value — the integrality gap, at most 4/3 conjecturally for metric instances. The "prism" example shows a gap of 18 vs 15; the ten-city worksheet instance is tight. Uniform growth is the greedy primal–dual alternative: all zones grow at the same speed until an edge becomes tight, whereupon the two zones merge. It always produces a valid laminar picture (worth the MST plus its longest edge), usually much weaker than the LP.

Input — distance matrix or Euclidean points, plus optional tour and moats
Random

Cities. First line: the number of cities n. Then n lines name x y — the name may contain spaces, the last two tokens are the coordinates (screen coordinates: y grows downwards). Cities are numbered 0 … n−1 in this order.

Distances. Either matrix followed by the upper triangle (row i lists d(i,j) for j > i, like a worksheet table) or the full n×n matrix; or euclid s [k]: distances are the Euclidean distances times s, rounded to k decimals (default 0) — nearly Euclidean, but not exactly, so geometric intuition applies while the numbers stay tidy. In euclid mode, dragging a city re-solves live.

Drawing an instance. Blank plane (or the Edit cities toggle on any instance) turns the map into a drawing board: click to place a city, drag to move it, alt-click to delete it. Distances are the pixel distances divided by the unit and rounded; a coarse unit gives small integers and visible rounding. The text below always mirrors the drawing, so a drawn instance can be copied out and pasted back later.

Drawing scale. Moat widths are in distance units; scale s sets how many coordinate units one distance unit is on the map. Without it the page fits s by least squares to the distance matrix. Embed (MDS) nudges the coordinates towards the matrix instead.
