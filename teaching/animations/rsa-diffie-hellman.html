Background — the problem, the arithmetic you need, and how to read this page

The problem

Alice wants to send Bob a private message, but every message travels through a channel that Eve can read — a postcard, a radio signal, the internet. For thousands of years secret writing needed a shared secret key agreed in advance: a code word exchanged in person, a courier with a sealed envelope. That does not scale to a world where you buy something from a shop you have never visited, on a phone you took out of a box this morning.

In 1976 Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman (with an idea of Ralph Merkle) showed how two people can agree on a secret number while shouting everything they say across a crowded room. In 1977 Ron Rivest, Adi Shamir and Leonard Adleman found a scheme where Bob publishes a "padlock" anyone can use to lock a message that only Bob's private key opens. (British government cryptographers had found the same ideas a few years earlier, in secret.) Both rest on one trick: arithmetic operations that are easy to do but seem impossible to undo.

Remainders: clock arithmetic

Everything here happens with remainders. \(a \bmod n\) means: divide \(a\) by \(n\), keep only the remainder — a number from 0 to \(n-1\). On a 12-hour clock, 9 o'clock plus 5 hours is 2 o'clock: that is \((9 + 5) \bmod 12 = 2\).

\(17 \bmod 5 = 2\)   because \(17 = 3 \cdot 5 + 2\)
\(65^2 \bmod 3233 = 4225 \bmod 3233 = 992\)   because \(4225 = 1 \cdot 3233 + 992\)

The one rule that makes this page possible: you may take the remainder whenever you like in the middle of a chain of additions and multiplications, and the final remainder does not change. Why? Write \(a = a' + k n\) and \(b = b' + l n\), where \(a', b'\) are the remainders. Then \[a \cdot b = a' b' + n\,(a' l + b' k + k l n).\] The second part is a multiple of \(n\), so it contributes nothing to the remainder: \(a \cdot b\) and \(a' \cdot b'\) leave the same remainder. Same story for addition.

Consequence: to compute \(x^k \bmod n\) we never need a number bigger than \(n^2\), even when \(x^k\) itself would have millions of digits. Combined with repeated squaring (square, square, square, and multiply in an extra factor where the binary digits of \(k\) say so) a power with a 77-digit exponent takes only about 250 multiplications. You will watch every one of them.

Divisibility words

  • A prime is a whole number \(\ge 2\) whose only divisors are 1 and itself: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, …
  • The greatest common divisor \(\gcd(a, b)\) is the largest number dividing both. If it is 1 we say \(a\) and \(b\) share no factor. Euclid's 2300-year-old algorithm finds it in a few lines.
  • The key fact about primes: if a prime divides a product \(a \cdot b\), then it divides \(a\) or it divides \(b\). (A composite number like 6 does not have this property: 6 divides \(4 \cdot 9 = 36\) but neither 4 nor 9.) The RSA tab proves this fact before using it.

One-way streets

Multiplying two 39-digit primes takes a computer a microsecond. Given only the 77-digit product, finding the two primes again — factoring — is enormously harder: this is what Eve would have to do to break RSA. Likewise, computing \(g^a \bmod p\) is quick, but given the result, finding the exponent \(a\) — the discrete logarithm — is hard: this is Eve's problem in Diffie–Hellman.

Honest fine print: nobody has proved that these problems are hard. What we know is that the best mathematicians and the best computers have tried for decades and the fastest known methods still need astronomical time when the numbers are large enough (real systems use 617-digit \(n\), not 77). The numbers this page calls "large" are large enough to be impressive, but a laptop running a clever factoring algorithm would still crack them in under an hour — the page tells you this again at the point where it matters. A large quantum computer would change the picture entirely (Shor's algorithm, 1994); none large enough exists, which is why the world is moving to "post-quantum" schemes right now.
