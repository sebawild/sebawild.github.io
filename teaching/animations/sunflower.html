Sunflowers  ·  the Erdös–Rado lemma and a kernel for d-Hitting Set

A sunflower is a family of sets that all meet in the same core, and are disjoint outside it. Erdős and Rado showed that every large family of small sets contains one, and their proof finds it: pick disjoint sets greedily; if there are too few of them, many sets must go through one element, so put that element into the core and repeat with the remaining sets. For d-Hitting Set, a sunflower with $k+1$ petals can be replaced by its core, because any $k$ elements that hit all petals must hit the core.

Sunflowers and a non-example
Definition

Sets $S_1,\ldots,S_r$ (all different) form a sunflower with $r$ petals and core $C$ if $S_i \cap S_j \wrel= C$ for all $i \ne j$.

Then the petals $S_i \setminus C$ are pairwise disjoint. $C = \emptyset$ is allowed: $r$ pairwise disjoint sets are a sunflower. At most one petal can be empty, namely if the core itself is one of the sets.

Sunflower Lemma (Erdős–Rado 1960)

Every family $\mathcal F$ of sets of size $\le d$ with $|\mathcal F| \wrel> d!\,(r-1)^d$ contains a sunflower with $r$ petals, and the proof finds it in polynomial time.

For $d = 2$ the bound says: a graph with more than $2(r-1)^2$ edges has $r$ disjoint edges or a vertex of degree $\ge r$.

How good is the bound?

Far from tight. Families of $(r-1)^d$ sets without a sunflower are easy to build (one element from each of $d$ groups of $r-1$), and $O(r \log d)^d$ sets always suffice (Alweiss, Lovett, Wu and Zhang 2019; Rao; Bell, Chueluecha and Warnke). Whether $c_r^{\,d}$ sets suffice is the Erdős–Rado sunflower conjecture, still open.

One branching step on a sunflower of clauses
The branching step

Clauses are sets of literals. Let the clauses $S_1,\ldots,S_r$ of $\mathcal F$ form a sunflower with core $C$, and let $G$ be the other clauses.

A satisfying assignment either satisfies $C$, and then all of $S_1,\ldots,S_r$; or it does not, and then it must satisfy every petal $S_i \setminus C$. Hence $\mathcal F \equiv \mathcal F_1 \lor \mathcal F_2$. In $\mathcal F_1$, $r$ clauses become one shorter clause; in $\mathcal F_2$, all $r$ clauses get shorter.

Sparsification Lemma (Impagliazzo–Paturi–Zane 2001)

For every $k \ge 3$ and $\varepsilon > 0$ there is a constant $K$ such that every $k$-CNF $\mathcal F$ on $n$ variables is equivalent to $\mathcal F_1 \lor \cdots \lor \mathcal F_t$ with $t \le 2^{\varepsilon n}$, where each $\mathcal F_i$ is a $k$-CNF on the same variables with at most $K n$ clauses. The $\mathcal F_i$ can be computed in time $2^{\varepsilon n}\, n^{O(1)}$.

Where the sunflowers come from

Branch as long as some set of literals lies in too many clauses of the same width. Removing it from these clauses leaves many small sets, so by the Sunflower Lemma they contain a large sunflower, and with the set put back it is a sunflower of clauses. At the end, every literal lies in $O(1)$ clauses, so each branch has $O(n)$ clauses. The work is in choosing the thresholds so that only $2^{\varepsilon n}$ branches arise.

Pseudocode
Input
A set family in the style of the algs4 graph format: the number of elements n, the number of sets m, then m lines, one per set, listing its elements (elements are 0…n−1; a line - is the empty set). A graph in algs4 format is such a family with two elements per line. Optionally n more lines x y give the drawing: any scale, y pointing down, the picture keeps its aspect ratio. Without them the elements are placed automatically.

A line k 2 (or k = 2) anywhere sets the parameter. The sets have size at most d, the largest set size, unless a line d 3 sets a larger d. Lines starting with # are comments. A set listed twice is kept once.

The order of the sets matters: the greedy pass of the lemma scans them in this order, and new cores are appended at the end. At most 60 elements and 150 sets.
